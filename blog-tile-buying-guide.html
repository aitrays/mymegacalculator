Tile Calculator Guide: How to Measure and Buy the Right Amount

By Alex Morgan · August 2026 · 9 min read

Buying tiles for a flooring or wall project sounds simple until you realize that running out halfway through installation is a nightmare — and buying too many means wasting hundreds of dollars. The difference between a smooth project and a stressful one often comes down to one thing: accurate measurement and calculation. In this guide, we will show you exactly how to measure any room for tiles, calculate the precise number you need, add the correct waste margin, and avoid the most common mistakes homeowners make.

Step 1: Measure the Room Accurately

Before you touch a calculator, you need reliable measurements. Use a quality tape measure — ideally a laser measure for large rooms — and record the length and width of the space in the same unit system you plan to use for your tiles. Most tile calculators accept either metric (meters and centimeters) or imperial (feet and inches).

Important: Measure wall-to-wall at the widest points. Do not assume walls are perfectly straight, especially in older homes. Measure at the floor level, not at waist height, as walls can bow outward or inward.

  • Measure the length and width of the room.
  • For L-shaped rooms, divide the space into rectangles and measure each separately.
  • Subtract the area of fixed objects (kitchen islands, bathtubs, built-in cabinets) if they will not be tiled underneath.
  • Do not forget closets, alcoves, and entryways — these add significant area.

Step 2: Know Your Tile Size

Tile sizes vary dramatically, and the size you choose affects how many you need. Common sizes include:

Tile SizeTypical UseArea per Tile
30×30 cmSmall bathrooms, mosaics900 cm² (0.09 m²)
60×60 cmStandard floors, living rooms3,600 cm² (0.36 m²)
80×80 cmLarge open spaces6,400 cm² (0.64 m²)
30×60 cmWalls, subway layouts1,800 cm² (0.18 m²)

Larger tiles cover more area per piece, which means fewer total tiles. However, larger tiles also generate more waste when cutting around corners, pipes, and irregular edges.

Step 3: Calculate Base Tile Count

Total Tiles = Room Area ÷ Area of One Tile

For a room measuring 5 meters by 4 meters (20 m² total) using 60×60 cm tiles (0.36 m² each):

20 m² ÷ 0.36 m² = 55.55 tiles → round up to 56 tiles

Always round up. You cannot buy 0.55 of a tile, and rounding down guarantees a shortage.

Step 4: Add the Waste Margin

This is where most DIYers make mistakes. Cutting tiles to fit edges, corners, and fixtures always produces waste. Broken tiles during transport or installation are also common. The waste percentage depends on your layout complexity:

  • 5% waste: Perfectly square room, straight grid layout, no obstacles.
  • 10% waste: Standard room with a few corners and one doorway. (Recommended default)
  • 15% waste: Diagonal or herringbone pattern, multiple cutouts.
  • 20% waste: Complex room with pillars, curved walls, or many fixtures.

For our 20 m² room with a standard 10% waste margin: 56 tiles × 1.10 = 61.6 → 62 tiles.

Step 5: Convert to Boxes

Tiles are sold in boxes, not individually. A typical box contains 4 to 10 tiles depending on size. Check the box label for the exact count and total coverage area. Divide your total tile count by tiles per box, and round up again.

62 tiles ÷ 6 tiles per box = 10.33 → buy 11 boxes

Pro Tips from Contractors

💡 Buy extra boxes for repairs

Keep 1–2 unopened boxes after installation. Tile dye lots change over time, and matching a broken tile two years later is nearly impossible.

💡 Check the dye lot number

Tiles from different production batches can have slight color variations. Buy all boxes from the same lot number.

💡 Dry lay before you glue

Lay out a few rows without adhesive first to check spacing, pattern alignment, and edge cuts.

Plan the Layout Before Ordering

Measure the room, mark the center lines, and decide where full tiles should be visible. A layout that begins with a narrow sliver at a doorway can look unbalanced, so shift the starting line when possible. Record tile length, width, grout joint, and pattern direction. For a 12 ft by 10 ft room using 12 inch square tile, the base area is 120 sq ft; with 10% cutting waste, order for 132 sq ft. The final package count must be rounded up to complete boxes.

Waste Is Not One Fixed Number

Straight layouts usually create less waste than diagonal, herringbone, or staggered patterns. Extra material is also valuable for future repairs because dye lots and product lines can change. A room with many corners, cabinets, or angled walls needs a careful cut plan. Do not hide unusable pieces inside the waste percentage: tiles with cracks, shade variation, or unacceptable warping should be rejected before installation.

Installation Checks

  • Confirm that the substrate is clean, dry, flat, and strong enough.
  • Buy all boxes from the same lot when color consistency matters.
  • Dry-lay rows to inspect the pattern and verify grout spacing.
  • Keep a labeled spare box for matching repairs.

Tile Buying FAQs

Do I calculate wall tile differently? No; calculate length × height for each wall, then subtract untiled openings. Should a box be rounded up? Always purchase whole boxes. Does grout change the area? It changes spacing, but tile still needs to cover the surface. Use the tile calculator, compare its result with package coverage, and see the square footage guide for room measurement.

Compare Samples and Specifications

View samples in the room’s actual light because screens and showroom lighting can alter color. Check the tile’s recommended location, water absorption, slip rating, thickness, and maintenance instructions. A wall product may not be suitable for a floor, and a polished surface may be inappropriate where water is regularly present. Ask how many pieces are in a box and whether the printed coverage already assumes a grout joint.

Keep a small diagram showing the room outline, start point, and difficult cuts. It helps an installer explain where full tiles and visible edges will land. Before ordering, compare the calculator’s area with the box coverage and round up to complete cartons. Keep the receipt, lot number, and one unopened box until the installation is accepted. That record is more useful than a larger arbitrary waste percentage.

Planning Tile Layout and Waste

Measure each room separately and sketch the direction of the longest wall. A centered layout often looks balanced, but it can create narrow cuts at both edges. Shift the starting line so the smallest border piece is not a thin sliver. Keep the finished tile size, grout joint, trim, and door thresholds in the plan because the visible floor area is not the only dimension that matters.

Worked example with a pattern

A 12 ft by 10 ft room has 120 square feet. If a 12 in by 24 in tile covers 2 square feet, the theoretical quantity is 120 ÷ 2 = 60 tiles. Add 10% for a straight layout: 60 × 1.10 = 66 tiles. A diagonal, herringbone, or room with many corners may need 15% or more. Buy from the same lot and keep a few spare tiles for future repairs.

Installation Checks

Confirm that the subfloor is clean, dry, flat, and strong enough for the selected tile. Mark reference lines with a chalk line, dry-lay a few rows, and check the manufacturer’s adhesive and grout guidance. Let the layout cross a doorway intentionally rather than ending with an accidental thin strip. Photograph the lot number and store extra material in a dry place.

Common buying mistakes

  • Ordering only the exact calculated area leaves no allowance for cuts or breakage.
  • Mixing boxes can create visible shade differences between production lots.
  • Tile coverage printed on a box may assume a particular grout width.
  • For walls, calculate each elevation and subtract openings only when the measurement is reliable.

FAQs and Useful Links

Should waste be added before or after division? Find the theoretical quantity first, then multiply by the waste factor and round up to whole boxes. How many spare tiles should remain? Keep enough to replace the most exposed area, usually at least one unopened box when storage allows.
