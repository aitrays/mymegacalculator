Understanding BMR, TDEE, and Calorie Math

Managing body weight is fundamentally governed by the energy balance equation: calories consumed versus calories expended.

1. Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)

The energy your body expends at rest to keep your heart beating, lungs breathing, and cells regenerating. Calculated via the Mifflin-St Jeor formula.

2. Total Daily Energy (TDEE)

Your BMR multiplied by your physical activity multiplier. Eating at your TDEE keeps your body weight steady.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between BMR and TDEE? ▼

Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is your baseline energy burn at complete rest. Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) adds all exercise, walking, and daily activity to your BMR.

How many calories should I eat to lose 1 lb per week? ▼

Consuming approximately 500 calories below your TDEE each day creates a weekly deficit of 3,500 calories, which equals roughly 1 pound (0.45 kg) of fat loss.

How to Interpret a Calorie Estimate

Enter age, sex, height, weight, and activity level using consistent units. The calculator estimates resting energy needs and then applies an activity factor to estimate daily maintenance calories. This is a planning starting point, not a measurement of metabolism. Body composition, sleep, illness, medication, training load, and the accuracy of the inputs can all change actual needs.

Formula and example

The common Mifflin-St Jeor equations estimate resting needs from weight, height, and age, with a sex-specific constant, then multiply by an activity factor. A 30-year-old person weighing 70 kg and measuring 175 cm will receive a different estimate at sedentary and very active settings. Change one input at a time and record the units so the comparison is meaningful.

Practical use

Use the maintenance estimate as a range and watch the trend over two to four weeks rather than reacting to one day. Food labels, restaurant portions, cooking oils, drinks, and weekend meals are frequent sources of undercounting. If adjusting body weight, make gradual changes and include protein, fiber, hydration, and enjoyable foods. Athletes, teenagers, pregnant people, and anyone with a medical condition should seek individualized advice.

Mistakes and limitations

Do not enter pounds where kilograms are requested, select an activity level that describes your aspiration rather than your routine, or treat the result as a diagnosis. Calories are not the only measure of nutrition quality, and the estimate cannot assess allergies or nutrient deficiencies. Recheck your inputs and consult a qualified professional when a large change is needed.

Making the estimate useful

Use a consistent weighing routine and compare weekly trends, because water, sodium, digestion, and exercise can move scale weight from one day to the next. Recalculate after a meaningful change in body weight or routine, but do not chase every fluctuation. The safest interpretation is a starting range that you test against real observations and adjust gradually with qualified guidance.

Using a Calorie Estimate Responsibly

A calorie estimate is a starting point, not a diagnosis or a prescription. The calculator uses age, sex setting, height, weight, and activity to estimate basal energy needs and daily maintenance. Your actual needs can change with training, sleep, medication, pregnancy, illness, and changes in body composition. Use a consistent method for several weeks and discuss major changes with a qualified professional.

Formula and worked example

A common resting-energy equation is Mifflin–St Jeor. For men, BMR = 10 × weight in kg + 6.25 × height in cm − 5 × age + 5. For women, replace the final constant with −161. Convert pounds to kilograms by multiplying by 0.453592 and inches to centimeters by multiplying by 2.54. If a 30-year-old man weighs 80 kg and is 180 cm tall, the estimate is 800 + 1125 − 150 + 5 = 1,780 kcal per day before activity.

Activity Factors and Mistakes

Multiplying BMR by an activity factor estimates maintenance, but a desk job with occasional exercise should not be treated like daily hard training. Log meals honestly, use the same weighing routine, and avoid changing several variables at once. Calories are not interchangeable with nutrition quality: protein, fiber, hydration, and micronutrients still matter.

How to use the calorie calculator well

Use your age, height, weight, and activity level as an honest starting point rather than trying to choose the most flattering option. The result estimates maintenance calories, often called total daily energy expenditure (TDEE). The formula behind the estimate

Many calculators first estimate basal metabolic rate (BMR) with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. For men, BMR is 10 times weight in kilograms plus 6.25 times height in centimeters, minus 5 times age, plus 5. For women, the final constant is minus 161. TDEE is then approximately BMR multiplied by an activity factor. For example, a 30-year-old woman at 65 kg and 165 cm has a BMR near 1,370 calories; a moderately active factor of 1.55 produces roughly 2,120 maintenance calories.

Practical steps

  1. Measure body weight under similar conditions, preferably in the morning.
  2. Choose the activity level that describes your normal week, not an occasional hard workout.
  3. Compare the estimate with a seven-day average of your food intake and weight trend.
  4. Adjust gradually after two or three weeks if your goal and trend do not match.

Common mistakes and safety notes

Do not double-count exercise by selecting a high activity factor and then automatically eating back every watch calorie. Packaged-food labels and restaurant portions also introduce error. A small calorie deficit is usually easier to sustain than an aggressive restriction, while athletes may need more carbohydrate around training. Children, pregnant or breastfeeding people, and anyone managing diabetes or an eating disorder should use individualized advice from a qualified clinician.
